Mike's Balance · editorial draft

Would you be better off in another country?

A country can be richer, safer, healthier or happier on average without giving a newcomer the full advantage. The useful question is not which country ranks higher, but which parts of life actually change when you move.

Relocation reality check · 54 countries · country-level evidence, not a personal forecast

A Finn who moves to Spain does not become a statistical Spaniard at passport control. The person arrives with an income source, a work arrangement, a health history, habits, expectations and a social network. At the same time, prices, institutions and the surrounding labour market really do change.

That distinction turned out to be the central problem in building this calculator. Relocation is not a comparison between two average residents. It is a transfer problem.

The question: what travels with you, what the destination changes immediately, and what appears to move only partly or over time?

A move is not a country swap

The first version of this project followed the obvious logic of country rankings. Compare income, prices, work, happiness, health, safety and institutions; then decide which side looks better. The problem is that national averages describe populations, while a migrant is the same person observed in a new environment.

Once we treated relocation that way, the indicators separated into three different classes.

What kind of variable?ExamplesWhat happens after a move?
PortableRemote income, existing jobMay travel almost unchanged unless the move itself alters the arrangement.
Destination exposurePrices, local labour market, administration, everyday safetyThe new environment matters quickly, although country averages remain coarse proxies.
Adaptive or accumulatedLife evaluation, healthOrigin history remains relevant. Some outcomes converge partly; others cannot be assigned a defensible universal transfer rate.

This is why the calculator has no overall “better country” score. The mechanism is different from card to card, and adding the numbers together would hide exactly what we are trying to learn.

What follows you: the same move can reverse its financial sign

Finland → Spain is a useful stress test because the answer changes before we touch happiness, health or safety. In the frozen data snapshot used by the calculator, keeping the same spendable income produces about 34% more purchasing power in Spain because the general price level is lower. But if the person instead enters the destination economy and we substitute Spain's broad GNI-per-capita PPP benchmark for Finland's, the comparison becomes about 11% lower.

Neither number predicts an individual's salary. The first is a price-level calculation under a strong assumption: spendable income is portable. The second is deliberately crude, using World Bank GNI per capita at purchasing-power parity as a broad country benchmark rather than pretending we know the person's occupation, employer, tax status or bargaining power. [1]

We tested a more personalised route using OECD Taxing Wages 2026, which models taxes and cash benefits for eight household types and different earnings levels. Household composition sometimes changed our test comparisons enough to reverse the sign. [3] That made family structure scientifically relevant, but it also exposed a coverage problem: a sophisticated OECD model cannot be applied consistently to a global set of destinations.

The current calculator therefore asks less. It distinguishes portable income, local income and income from pension/capital/savings. A previously tested 50/50 mixed scenario was removed because “half portable” was a convenient convention, not an observed fact.

What the destination changes quickly

Some conditions really are encountered after arrival. Consumer prices are paid where you live. A local employee enters the destination labour market. Administrative systems become the systems you must use. The local environment also changes exposure to public safety and healthcare institutions.

That does not make the national indicators personal predictions. Gallup's safety measure, for example, asks whether people feel safe walking alone at night where they live. It captures an everyday perception of security, not recorded crime, and national averages conceal large neighbourhood and subgroup differences. [4] The calculator therefore shows the change in percentage points rather than presenting safety as a personal probability.

“Bureaucracy” is even more explicitly a proxy. We use the World Bank's Government Effectiveness measure, which combines perception-based evidence about public services, civil-service quality, policy implementation and government credibility. The World Bank cautions that these are estimates with margins of error, not definitive ratings of a government or of one immigration office. [5]

Out-of-pocket healthcare spending is kept separate from general prices for the same reason. It reflects not only prices but also utilisation and health-system design. A country can be cheaper overall while its residents spend more directly on healthcare. Combining the two into a single cost-of-living score would erase that distinction. [6]

What appears to move partly: life evaluation

Subjective wellbeing is where the transfer problem becomes more interesting. A country happiness ranking describes people already living there. It does not tell us what happens to someone who arrives from somewhere else.

Our earlier Mike's Balance investigation, “Can a Country Make You Happier?”, showed why the word happiness needs care. The World Happiness Report's Cantril Ladder is an overall evaluation of life, not a count of pleasant emotions. Life satisfaction, direct feelings of happiness and yesterday's positive or negative experiences can produce noticeably different country patterns even when they all sound like “happiness” in casual language. [7]

Migration studies nevertheless suggest that life evaluation is not fixed at the origin-country level. The World Happiness Report 2018 found that immigrant life evaluation depended strongly on destination-country levels, describing a rough destination weight of about three-quarters in its cross-country analysis. Its matched comparison also estimated an average gain of 0.47 points on the 0–10 Cantril Ladder among international migrants, while explicitly noting that many migrants did not gain. [8]

Helliwell, Shiplett and Bonikowska reached a similar broad conclusion using immigrants to Canada and the United Kingdom: immigrant life-satisfaction distributions looked much more like those of destination populations than those of source populations. [9]

We then used our own analysis as a stress test rather than as the foundation of the claim. In an exploratory reanalysis at The Null Institute, we pooled European Social Survey rounds 1–11. In a stricter adult post-1991 specification, 20 origin-destination routes involving 2,361 migrants produced a life-satisfaction destination-transfer slope of 0.662, with a route-bootstrap 95% interval of 0.564–0.844. [10]

The interpretation is descriptive. A slope of zero would place migrant-group life satisfaction at the origin level; one would place it at the destination level. Roughly two-thirds means substantial destination alignment, not that “the country causes 66% of happiness.” Selection into migration, destination choice and selective return remain unresolved.

We also tested a measurement-based alternative: perhaps migrants simply learn to use 0–10 response scales the way destination residents do. Response style itself did converge, especially endpoint use, but within-person standardisation and rank-based transformations did not remove the destination signal. That makes pure response-style assimilation an incomplete explanation, not a causal proof of environmental effects. [10]

A rare natural experiment is useful precisely because it refuses to make the story neat. In the Tonga → New Zealand migration lottery, successful migrants gained substantially on several objective outcomes and mental health, yet one direct happiness measure declined relative to unsuccessful lottery applicants. [11] Different dimensions of wellbeing can move in opposite directions.

The calculator therefore uses 0.65 as a rough group-level convergence heuristic for life evaluation. It is close to our ESS estimate and broadly compatible with earlier migration evidence. But it remains a modelling convention, and it is applied to World Happiness Report Cantril scores even though the ESS coefficient comes mainly from a life-satisfaction question. The broad direction is much more defensible than the second decimal place.

Health is where the transfer model fails

Once happiness had a rough convergence coefficient, it was tempting to give health one too. We could take the destination's healthy life expectancy and move the migrant some percentage of the way toward it. That would make the calculator beautifully symmetrical.

We rejected it.

WHO healthy life expectancy (HALE) is a population measure combining mortality and morbidity. In our frozen snapshot, Spain's HALE is about 1.2 years higher than Finland's. [12] That is useful information about the two populations. It is not evidence that a Finnish adult gains 1.2 healthy years by moving to Spain, nor that some fixed fraction of those years should be credited after migration.

The migrant-health literature is too heterogeneous for a universal transfer coefficient. A systematic review of 78 Canadian studies found that the “healthy immigrant effect” varied by stage of life and outcome, was stronger for mortality than morbidity, and tended to be stronger among more recent immigrants. The authors explicitly concluded that there was no uniform foreign-born health advantage across outcomes and the life course. [13]

Health therefore stays in the calculator as population context. Unlike happiness, it receives no migrant adjustment. The model becomes less elegant at exactly the point where the evidence becomes less uniform.

Where people actually live abroad

After choosing an origin country, the calculator shows five common destinations using the UN DESA International Migrant Stock 2024 bilateral dataset. [14] The word stock matters: it describes where migrants from an origin are living, not where people moved during 2024 and not which destinations are currently “best”. Historical diasporas, changing borders and older migration waves can dominate the ranking.

If one of the real top-five destinations is outside the calculator's 54-country coverage, it remains visible but disabled. Replacing it with the next supported country would make the interface cleaner and the data less true.

Why we stopped adding questions

A more personal calculator could ask about household composition, occupation, age, education, city, visa status, tax residence, political preferences, health conditions and climate tolerance. Many of those variables genuinely matter. That is exactly why casually adding them is dangerous.

An input earns its place only if it has a plausible mechanism, can materially alter the result and is supported by comparable data across most destinations. Household structure passed the first two tests but not the global-coverage test. Several other tempting inputs failed even earlier.

The aim is therefore not maximum personalisation. It is the smallest model that makes the important assumptions visible.

How to read the result

The calculator is best used as a contradiction detector. If every card points in the same direction, the move may deserve closer attention. More often, the interesting result is that the cards disagree.

A person keeping a remote salary may gain purchasing power in a lower-price country while entering a population with lower average life evaluation. Someone taking a local job may get the opposite financial answer. A destination may have higher healthy life expectancy but worse perceived safety, or lower general prices but higher out-of-pocket healthcare spending.

No statistical rule can decide how much money is worth one point of safety or one year of population HALE. Producing a grand score would require value judgements disguised as mathematics.

The more useful output is therefore a map of trade-offs, with each number labelled according to what kind of evidence it actually represents: a direct scenario calculation, a destination exposure, a group-level migration heuristic or population context.

The question that remains

The broad lesson is that country advantage and migration effect are different quantities. The next scientific problem is to estimate transferability more directly: for each outcome, how much of the origin-destination gap is experienced by migrants, on what timescale, and through which mechanism?

Life evaluation gives us evidence of substantial but incomplete convergence. Health warns us that no universal rule should be assumed. Income shows that the answer can change sign depending on what the migrant carries across the border.

That may be less satisfying than a ranking of the world's best places to live. It is much closer to the question a person actually has when deciding whether to move.
